kwin-mcp-warden · Design proposal 1 · The consent prompt

Claude asks before it touches your desktop

kwin-mcp lets Claude see, move, click and type on your KDE desktop. kwin-mcp-warden keeps every one of those tools and adds a warden in front of them. Before Claude gets an app, you see a prompt that names who is asking, what for, what it would allow and for how long. While Claude has it, the window says so, your hands come first, and one key takes it all back.

What changes

From silent access to a prompt you answer

Today, any Claude session that loads kwin-mcp can click anywhere on your screen without asking. Its one guard, --live-consent, covers only closing a window. The warden sits between Claude's request and KWin, and nothing passes it without a grant you gave.

  • 1A prompt Claude can't answer. The warden draws it, not Claude, and holds all of Claude's input while it's open. It opens front and center on your primary monitor and dims every screen behind it. Only a click allows.
  • 2Grants per window, in four levels. See, Arrange, Control, or Full desktop. Claude asks for the least it needs in one window, and the card names that window by its title and shows how much on a four-step scale. Claude sees nothing of any other window, not even the same app's.
  • 3Visible while it lasts. The window Claude controls has an orange frame and a tag with a Stop button. Your pointer carries a "Claude" label while Claude moves it.
  • 4Your hands come first. Click or press a key and Claude pauses. A nudged mouse pauses Claude only while it uses the pointer. It continues after 3 seconds without your input.
  • 5Grants end. Each grant ends when the session ends or after 1 hour, whichever comes first. Meta+Alt+Esc ends every grant at once, and so does the access list.
  • 6Some things are never granted. The warden's own prompt, password prompts and the lock screen can't be granted. Closing a window asks every time.
The request

One card, front and center

Here, the Mail App session needs to test its sign-in page in your Chrome. The card opens in the middle of your primary monitor, and every screen dims behind it, as Windows does for its UAC prompt. While it's up, the warden holds the keyboard: no window behind it gets your keys, and no key answers it except Esc, which means Don't Allow. The card is drawn in your KDE theme (its window color, Noto Sans, its text and warning colors), with our own parts inside it, composed around one sentence: Claude asks to control a Google Chrome window.

Open and control New

An app that isn't open: one card opens it and allows it

Before any card, the warden checks what's open. If the app has a window on your desktop, the card asks for the level in that window, named by its title. If it isn't open, one card asks to open it and allow the level in its new window together. Allow does both; Don't Allow does neither. A card never asks about an app you can't see.

  • 1Kate is open. The card you know: control Kate, for 1 hour.
  • 2Kate isn't open. The title says both things, and the line under the reason says what Allow does first. The level, what it means and how long it lasts read as in the first card.

While Claude has it

The window says who has it

Allowed. The Chrome window gets an orange frame, and a tag above it names the session and the time left, with a Stop button. The orange is Claude Code's own, the color your terminal already shows for Claude, so Claude's hand reads as Claude's at a glance. Your KDE accent keeps its own jobs, focus and selection. While Claude moves the pointer, a "Claude" label rides it.

In control

One place to stop it, always in the corner

While any session controls anything on your screen, a notice sits in the top-right corner of your primary monitor: who is in control, of what, for how long, and one button that ends it, with its key beside it. The tag above a window can be covered or half off a screen, and Full desktop has no single window to frame; the corner never moves. Reaching for the button is your input, so Claude has already paused by the time you get there. Top right, because Plasma's notifications and your tray live bottom right.

Your hands come first

Click or type and Claude waits

There is one pointer and one keyboard focus, and you both use them, so you take turns. A click or a key press from you pauses Claude at once, mid-action. Moving the mouse pauses Claude only while Claude is clicking, dragging or scrolling, so it never pulls the pointer out from under you (the fix for November 3). Claude's typing goes on under a nudged mouse, because a nudge can't send its keys anywhere else. Claude continues after 3 seconds without your input, and only once the window it holds has focus again.

Accessibility New

Claude types into the field, not at your focus

Typing with keys goes wherever your keyboard focus is, and you share that focus with Claude. That's why Claude pauses when you click or type. There is a second way in. Through accessibility, the channel screen readers use, Claude can write straight into a text field in the app you allowed, even when its window is behind yours. Nothing moves, your focus stays where it is, and your typing stays yours. It works in Kate, Konsole, Dolphin and every KDE, Qt and GTK app. Chrome, apps built on it, and Firefox can't be written into this way, so there Claude keeps typing at your focus, with the pauses.

For apps already open on your desktop, it needs your desktop's accessibility switch. That switch is off on your desktop now. KDE has no plain switch for it in System Settings: its Screen Reader toggle starts Orca, which talks. So the warden flips the switch itself, and only after you allow it on a card.

  • 1Asked once, when it's first needed. The first time Claude reads or writes a text field and the switch is off, this card asks. Claude still needs a grant for each app; this card only opens the channel. If you keep it off, Claude types at your focus as before, and doesn't ask again this session.
  • 2What it means, in plain words. Apps describe their windows and text fields to assistive tools. Nothing changes on screen.
  • 3The risk. While it's on, any program running as you can read what your apps show. That's true of every desktop with a screen reader on, and it's why the switch goes back off.
  • 4How long, on the button. "Turn On While Claude Works": it goes back off when Claude's last grant ends, on Meta+Alt+Esc, or from the access list.

The other asks

Some actions ask every time, and some are never allowed

A grant covers a level of work in one window, with the dialogs and menus that open from it. Some actions are too final to cover in advance, so they get their own card each time. Some places are never handed over, whatever Claude asks.

Asks every time

Closing a window, especially with unsaved work. Raw D-Bus calls (kwin-mcp's dbus_call, which can reach anything on your session bus). Reading or setting your clipboard. Opening an app Claude doesn't already have.

Never granted

The warden's own prompt and access list. Password prompts (system, KWallet, polkit). The lock screen. The warden's settings. Claude gets a plain refusal, the attempt is logged, and you see no card.

The access list

Everything Claude holds, and the way to end it

The warden's icon in the system tray turns orange while any session holds a grant. Your bottom panel hides itself, so the frame on the window is the indicator you'll actually see. The list is where you review and end grants. Below the grants, the latest log lines. The full log is a plain file.

The levels

Four levels, granted per window

Claude asks for one level in one window of an app: the card names the app's window you used last that Claude doesn't hold yet, by its title, and Claude asks again for each other window. A window's grant covers its own dialogs and menus, and ends when the window closes. Each level includes the ones above it in this table. The prompt's lines come from this table.

Where Claude works

Your screen, or a screen of Claude's own

Real input goes to the screen you're looking at, so when Claude controls an app on your screen, you share the pointer and take turns. kwin-mcp can also start a separate desktop in a window on the session's own desktop, with its own pointer and keyboard, where nothing can reach yours. Either way, Claude asks first, with the same card.

Claude's own screen needs permission

Claude shouldn't create its own virtual desktops unless it gets permission from the consent card. A screen of Claude's own is a grant like any other: same card, same time limit, listed in Claude's access, ended by Meta+Alt+Esc. Once you allow it, Claude may do its work there.

Building it

What the fork adds, and how it gets checked

PartWhat it does
The wardenSits in front of every kwin-mcp tool. Each call is checked against the grants, or opens a card and waits. A tool missing from the levels table is refused, so a new upstream tool is closed until it's placed.
The card and frameA small Qt Quick overlay (layer shell) above every window on every screen: the dim on each one, the card in the middle of the primary monitor, which is read from KDE rather than named in the code. Its window color, text, font and warning colors come from your KDE theme through Kirigami.Theme at run time, so a different theme follows; the shapes, buttons, scale and Claude's orange are its own.
Your hands firstWatches for your input through KWin and holds Claude's queued events while you're active. Claude's own events come in through its own input connection, so KWin can tell them apart. That is not proven yet. A small test inside a nested KWin comes first, before anything else is built.
SettingsA plain file you can edit: maximum grant time, the pause time, the stop key, the warning list and the never list.
The logOne line per request and per answer, plus every refused event, in ~/.local/state/kwin-mcp-warden/log.
Boundary testsEach run inside a private nested KWin, never on your desktop. Each test tries to cross a limit from outside: a click on a window that wasn't granted, a click while a card is open, a key while you're typing, a tool not in the table, a grant past its hour. Each one is first proven to catch a planted fault.
